**The experiment, and its answer is unambiguous.**

    the reference photograph        pole +0.1049   surround -0.1093   EXCESS **+0.2142**   cooler
    through the chain               pole +0.0009   surround -0.2007   EXCESS **+0.2016**   cooler
    chain, light mechanisms at zero pole +0.0128   surround -0.2391   EXCESS **+0.2519**   cooler
    chain, exposure -0.6            pole +0.0516   surround -0.1406   EXCESS **+0.1922**   cooler

**The reference photograph's pole stays cooler than its surround in every configuration -- it never inverts.** So the
chain does **not** do this to poles as a class, and the fault is **not** in the chain's handling of a bright thin
structure. **The difference lies between the two pictures.**

**And comparing the two subjects explains it, with numbers rather than inference:**

    refscene.png (the user's photograph)   pole +0.0062   surround -0.0301   EXCESS +0.0363
    the reference photograph               pole +0.1049   surround -0.1093   EXCESS **+0.2142**

**The reference's pole carries nearly six times the excess**, and -- more to the point -- **its surround is genuinely
cooler than the pole in the source**, so the chain has a clear difference to preserve. In the reproduction's photograph
the pole's own excess is only **+0.0363** to begin with, and the chain's own additions shift the **surround** upward by
around **0.06**, which is **larger than the entire difference it was supposed to preserve.** **A difference smaller
than the perturbation cannot survive it**, which is why every attempt to tune the perturbation down cost something else
and why no setting reached the target.

**So this closes the line of enquiry with a diagnosis rather than a fix**, and the diagnosis is the same one that
appeared twice before in this work: **a subject-scale mismatch**. The `source_glow` coefficient was two orders of
magnitude out between a test square and a real lamp; the pole's scattering region could not be matched without a light
source filling a comparable part of the frame; and now the pole's **colour difference to its own surround is too small
in this photograph for the chain to preserve through its own additions.** The reference photograph has a pole whose
colour contrast is six times larger, which is why it survives.

**What that means in practice, stated plainly:** the reproduction is not broken and its settings are not wrong -- **the
input photograph does not contain the pole-versus-surround colour contrast that the reference's contains, so no setting
of a chain that adds light can reproduce it.** The remedy is the same kind as before: **material with the property the
effect needs**, in this case a pole that is clearly cooler than what surrounds it in the source.

**And a note on the instrument, because it is what made this answer possible.** The detector was run **bounded by each
photograph's own pole geometry** and it located the reference's pole at **columns 904..913, 10 px wide, over 556
rows** -- independently, on a picture it had never seen, and with the positive control already establishing that it
returns "not found" on a bare wall and is defeated by a bright edge when unbounded. **That is the first time in this
line of work that a figure has been produced by an instrument whose failure modes were known in advance**, and it is
the reason this result can be believed where the earlier ones could not.

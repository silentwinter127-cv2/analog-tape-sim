**One claim was made and then refuted by its own control, and it is withdrawn.** NIGHTSHOT 2 against NIGHTSHOT 1 reads
**greener (0.4125 to 0.4439), 34% brighter (0.2532 to 0.3390) and 26% lower in detail (0.02309 to 0.01711)** -- and
those three moving together is the signature of **frame accumulation**, which implied **N = 1.82 from the noise law and
N = 2.25 from the motion**, converging on about 2 frames. **The model's preset uses 8, so it looked like a four-fold
error worth correcting.**

**The control refuted it.** The camera's own displacement was measured per segment by aligning consecutive frames:

    NIGHTSHOT 1   8.71 px per frame
    NIGHTSHOT 2   2.99 px per frame      the speed ratio is 0.343

**A camera moving at a third of the speed produces a third of the frame-to-frame change with no accumulation at all** --
and dividing the motion ratio 0.444 by the speed ratio 0.343 gives **1.296, which implies N = 0.77**. So the motion
ratio is **explained by the shooting** and is not evidence about the camera. **And the noise law cannot be measured from
one segment either**: across the frames of each, `corr(luma, hf)` is **+0.077** and **-0.030**, i.e. no noise signature.

**So what the two NightShot segments differ by is gain and white balance** -- 34% brighter and 0.031 greener with B
unchanged at 0.3543 -- **and the accumulation count is not determinable from this footage.** The preset stays at 8 for
now: **the honest state is that this clip does not constrain it**, and changing it on the strength of the refuted
estimate would have written a guess into the model as a calibration.

**The lesson, recorded because it is the second time this session**: a ratio between two *different takes* measures the
takes as much as the mechanism. The default's value is a separate question from whether the mechanism works, and the
mechanism's law was verified against a controlled moving source where the camera's speed was a known input.

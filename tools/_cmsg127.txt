measure the temporal response inside each segment: the settle after a mode change, and the emitter trail

**Both measurements are now possible because the segment boundaries are the operator's own.** Until they were, the
frames right after a change could not be identified -- and those frames are the only place a transient is visible, since
in the middle of a segment it is over. This was the item the earlier rounds left undone after failing to find cuts by
correlation threshold.

**1. The settle, and its control is what makes it readable.**

The level across the seven mode changes moves by **18.1% to 129.6%**; **inside a segment it moves by 0.2% to 2.9%** --
**an order of magnitude apart**, so a transient is a real event and not how this material varies. The control windows are
the middle of the first mode, of NIGHTSHOT 1 and of NIGHTSHOT 2.

The settle times, for the transitions whose step is well above the control:

    normal -> NightShot (short), 52 s    63% at +36 frames = 1.20 s
    NightShot -> ?, 53 s                 63% at +31 frames = 1.03 s
    NIGHTSHOT 2 -> after, 83 s           63% at +27 frames = 0.90 s
    NIGHTSHOT 1 -> NIGHTSHOT 2, 68 s     63% at +16 frames = 0.53 s
    NightShot -> normal, 45 s            63% at +12 frames = 0.40 s

**Three of them bracket the model's own automatic-exposure time constant of `tau_s = 1.1 s = 33 frames`** -- 1.20, 1.03
and 0.90 s -- which is a calibration the model did not have before.

**And the transitions overshoot**, which the control made measurable:

    the control's noise floor              2.65%, 6.22%, 3.41%
    NIGHTSHOT 2 -> after                   excursion 81.51% of the level
    NightShot -> ?                         48.99%
    ? -> NIGHTSHOT 1                       43.97%
    NightShot -> normal                    38.37%
    NIGHTSHOT 1 -> NIGHTSHOT 2             28.60%

**Five of the seven exceed three times the noise floor.** So the picture does not ease into its new operating point: it
**goes past it by 28-82% of its own level and comes back**. **That is a servo hunting, and it is the same behaviour as
the 1.60 s periodicity found earlier** -- which had been attributed to handheld sway, and is better explained by the
exposure loop oscillating in low light. **One claim from an earlier round is therefore improved, not merely added to.**

**2. And the overshoot statistic was wrong on its first attempt, with its own control proving it.**

It was defined as the largest excursion **as a fraction of the step**, which is sensible only when the step dominates
the noise -- and it was applied to control windows whose step is **±0.6%**. There the denominator *is* the noise, so the
ratio is unbounded:

    middle of the first mode      "+3.76"
    middle of NIGHTSHOT 1         "+13.34"
    middle of NIGHTSHOT 2         "+18.10"      in a window where nothing happened

**A statistic reporting +18 for an event of zero size is not measuring overshoot.** It was replaced by an excursion
**with the level as its denominator**, which is always of order one, and reported beside the control's own floor so a
value can be judged significant. **The lesson is the control's:** the same table that gives the result is the one that
exposed the instrument, and only because the control was measured and printed alongside.

**3. The emitter trail: none measurable, at 25.8 px per frame.**

    the fastest window of NIGHTSHOT 1, decoded at the full 1440x1080
    displacement between consecutive frames  (-15, -21) = 25.8 px, correlation 0.9640
    emitters averaged 405
    ahead 0.51856   behind 0.51525   behind-ahead -0.00332   ratio 0.994

**A single frame of persistence would smear about 26 px**, far beyond the ten-pixel profile window sampled -- so the
near-symmetry is **a result about the camera, not a resolution limit**. The profile is taken **along the measured
displacement direction**, so "behind" is defined by the data rather than assumed, and it is measured at the **source
resolution** because the previous attempt at 720x540 applied a 2x spatial filter to the quantity under test.

**And the earlier attempt's failure is now explained**: its window turned out to be two shots spliced, with **zero
displacement** (`dx = dy = 0`, correlations 1.0000). **A trail cannot exist without motion**, so it was measuring the
emitters' own shape -- and the "leading uplift" it reported (ratio 0.858) was that shape, not a temporal effect.

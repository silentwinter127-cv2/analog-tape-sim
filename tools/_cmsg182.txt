follow the YUV colour space, and the rainbow fringe turns out to have been there all along

**The user's instruction named the architectural fault, and correcting it changed the primary colour's structure.**  The
night-vision display expressed its cast as **RGB per-channel gains**, which cannot say what a display does: a green display
passes luminance almost unchanged and attenuates **its two chroma channels, each by its own factor**.  The RGB form is the
special case where U and V share a scale -- **which is why `chroma_residue` had to be invented, and why it then behaved
strangely: it carried chroma through a path built to carry gain.**

**The term now works in BT.601 as the standard defines it**, with `Y` the sensor's luminance and

     U = (B - Y) * 0.564      V = (R - Y) * 0.713

**and the direction of the two chroma gains is the whole mechanism.**  A green display is one whose blue channel is weak,
and the blue difference is `U` -- so the cast is **U suppressed harder than V**, which is `note.txt`'s `U减少 / V偏移` read
literally.  **My first version had them the other way round** (U 0.70, V 1.30) and measured **`R/G 1.000, B/G 1.000` on a
neutral input -- it produced GREY**, because passing more V than U pulls towards red while a green display pulls towards
green.  **A second correction was needed too**: the output was being built straight from the input's chroma, **discarding
the display projection** -- a neutral grey input came out `R/G 1.000, B/G 1.000`, the display's green replaced by the
input's neutrality.  The projection is now the base and the residue a perturbation on top.

**And the luminance is carried by identity rather than by argument**: Y is `lum` and the inverse re-solves G from the Y
equation, so **no scale on U or V can move the mean** -- verified, a neutral grey reads `luma 0.50000` at residue 0.00,
0.15, 0.45 and 1.00 exactly.  Every earlier version of this term had to *argue* the mean was held; this one gets it from
the algebra.

**The rainbow fringe had been present all along, and three faults of my own were hiding it:**

  * the measurement that first looked for it read the user's *exported* render -- a picture of the model **before** any
    change;
  * the second left `chroma_aberration` at **zero** along with the other optics, **so the mechanism that makes a fringe was
    switched off inside the rig testing for a fringe**;
  * and the third used the basement photograph, **which has no saturated emitters at all** -- measured, its brightest band
    holds **1,893 pixels against the real frame's 5,463 and 35,263**, so the statistic was being computed on a handful of
    pixels.

**On the case photograph -- the input that actually has RGB emitters -- the fringe is at the target, and my `0.05` default
was never the problem:**

    chroma_aberration   the brightest band's hue spread   pixels
    0.05 (the default)  0.988                             7353
    0.80                1.010                             7133
    1.20                1.017                             7067
    the real NIGHTSHOT 2  **1.093**                       699
    the real NIGHTSHOT 1  **1.035**                       3787

**so the fringe matches without a parameter being touched**, and the fix was to the rig rather than to the model.

**What is still out, and it is separable**: the basement renders `#24332E` with `G` largest, matching the footage's
structure -- but the **case** renders `#3A4A61` with **B largest**, against the footage's `#243E34` / `#344E45`.  **Measured,
that is a property of the input rather than of the chain**: the case photograph's own primary is `#373855` with a blue-to-
green ratio of **1.535**, so its blue, magenta and cyan emitters overwhelm the green the display adds -- and sweeping
`chroma_residue` from 0.15 to 0.60 moves its `B/G` only from **1.317 to 1.197**, i.e. the residue is not the control.  **The
display projection itself is behaving**: it produces `G` largest on a neutral field and on the basement.

**`tools/calib/FRINGE.png` is the test figure**, with the four brightness bands' hue spread **and their pixel counts** on
every panel, so a band computed on almost no pixels cannot be mistaken for a measurement.

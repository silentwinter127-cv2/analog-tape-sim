Three measurements, each one removing support from the previous conclusion.

**A tracker can fabricate the ratio by itself.** Synthetic fields with *identical* true
displacement spread (1.000 px) are reported as 0.841 px when smooth-dominated and 3.129 px
when fast-dominated -- a factor of 3.7 with no change in the underlying displacement at
all. The ratio had been measured with that same tracker on both stretches, so any
difference in their composition was being counted as deck behaviour.

**Composition does differ between the stretches, so a correction is needed -- and applying
it moved the figure the wrong way.** Reading a scale factor per stretch off the measured
response curve, the raw ratio of 0.44x becomes 0.52x. Small, but in the direction that
matters: the correction does not rescue the 8.8x, it further undermines it.

**And then the premise itself failed.** Scanning the per-row spread every five seconds
between 1000s and 1300s shows it flipping between roughly 0.1 and 3 px from one sample to
the next:

    1005s -> 1010s   0.345 -> 2.209   (x6.4)
    1010s -> 1015s   2.209 -> 0.159   (x0.07)
    1075s -> 1080s   1.877 -> 0.149   (x0.08)
    1090s -> 1095s   0.091 -> 1.100   (x12.1)
    1180s -> 1185s   0.205 -> 2.912   (x14.2)

That is not two stretches with a corrector switched between them. It is an *intermittent*
fault changing on a scale of seconds, which is also what the user's description said --
the TBC was being toggled repeatedly, so there was never a clean "off" window to measure
against. The 8.8x was the ratio between two arbitrary moments, and what it measured was
which moments were sampled.

This also retroactively explains why five attempts to measure the underlying component
kept failing: there is no single underlying component to measure on this material. Any
figure derived from it is a sample of a flickering signal.

What survives, and is worth keeping:

  * the tracker is validated and its non-linearity is quantified, so future figures from it
    can be corrected rather than trusted;
  * the fault's intermittency is a measured property, and a mechanism that scales smoothly
    cannot produce it -- which is a real constraint on the model, independent of the ratio;
  * the corrector fix itself is unaffected: it was found by reading the arithmetic
    (a correction that cancelled itself exactly), not by measuring this capture.

59 checks pass. The row-time-base amplitude is *not* calibrated against this material, and
should not be: it needs a capture with a stable, known state, or a controlled comparison
rather than two windows of a flickering fault.

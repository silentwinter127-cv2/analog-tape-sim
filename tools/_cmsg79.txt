trace the invented chroma to its true origin, and refute the fix I attempted

**Where it really comes from.** Earlier I recorded the mechanism as aliasing in `fft_bandpass`, on the strength of a
measurement showing the invented chroma fall from 40.1% to 0.2% as the sample rate rose. **That measurement was
wrong, and the way it was wrong matters.**

The composite was built with `composite_encode(..., std)` at the **original** standard and then split with a **copy**
of the standard whose `pixel_rate_hz` had been raised. `composite_encode` uses the standard's rate to generate the
subcarrier, so the two sides disagreed about what rate the signal was at -- and the fall from 40% to 0.2% was the
result of that disagreement, not of oversampling.

**Done properly, by giving both sides the same raised rate, the fraction does not move at all:**

    factor   pixel rate   samples/cycle   chroma returned   fraction of the I it was encoded with
    1.0      11.33        3.165           0.13790           40.1%     the model's rate
    2.0      22.66        6.330           0.13766           40.1%
    4.0      45.31       12.659           0.13725           40.0%
    8.0      90.63       25.319           0.13641           39.7%

**Forty per cent, at every rate up to eight times the model's.** So the invented chroma is **not** something the
bandpass picks out of the sampled spectrum -- it is **already in the composite**, produced by `composite_encode`
generating the subcarrier on the pixel grid. Raising the sample rate cannot recover information that was lost when
the signal was made, which is what the earlier "fix" was trying to do.

**So the fault is in the composite's construction**, and the arithmetic that decides it is unchanged and still the
right thing to look at: **3.17 samples per subcarrier cycle** at the model's rate, against the **4 samples per cycle**
that real digital NTSC chose (4 x fsc = 14.32 MHz) precisely so that the subcarrier is representable. If the
subcarrier is mixed at a rate where a cycle is barely three samples, the modulation gives a different result for
different line phases and **a constant field does not produce a constant composite** -- which is exactly what was
measured: the field's own composite has `mean 0.4378, std 0.153369`, where a field with no chroma must have a
**constant** composite.

**The attempted fix is reverted.** `VideoStandard.oversampled()` was added and is removed again: it is correct in
itself -- it keeps `pixels_for_bandwidth` consistent so a filter asked for a bandwidth in Hz behaves the same at
either rate -- but **it does not address this fault**, and shipping it would have been a change with no evidence
behind it. `standards.py` is byte-identical to its committed state.

**What the next attempt has to establish first**, because two failed attempts have both mis-stated the mechanism:
**why a field with no chroma produces a composite with a non-zero standard deviation.** That is a property of
`composite_encode` alone and can be measured in isolation -- a constant I of zero and a constant Q of zero must give a
flat plane -- without any filter or stage in the picture. The previous two attempts both reasoned about what happened
*after* the signal existed, and both were wrong for that reason.

**Three refutations and one self-correction this turn**, recorded because each closes a direction: the chroma band
limit, the per-line phase error and the luma frequency response are excluded by measurement; `vert_blend` is
unreachable on the path this preset takes; and the aliasing story above is withdrawn as an artefact of my own test.

Nothing is changed: `standards.py`, `signal.py` and `player.py` are all byte-identical to their committed state, and
163 checks pass.

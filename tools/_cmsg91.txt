add `source_glow`: a scattered halo carrying the source's own colour, and find what its calibration was really about

**The mechanism, and why it was missing.** Light from a bright source scattered in the air and in the glass keeps the
source's spectrum, so an orange lamp wears an orange halo and a white one a white halo. Measured against the
reference's own profile below a blown core:

    rows below    +60      +100     +160     +180
    the reference -0.170   -0.510   +0.178   +0.226
    without it    -0.193   -0.137   -0.072   -0.094

**Nothing in the module followed the source's colour.** `halation` adds a glow with a **fixed, cool** tint;
`lens_flare`'s tint is the **coating stack's**. A fixed tint is a constant and a source's colour is not, so neither
could be made to do this at any value. That is a mechanism that was missing rather than a parameter to tune, and the
evidence is the profile pair above.

**`atsim/codecs/lens.py: source_glow`** builds it from the highlight map, blurred **per channel** so each keeps its
own proportion, and added directly. Verified on synthetic sources of four colours: an orange source gives a halo at
**B-R -0.0027**, a white one **0.0000**, and a blue or red one follows its own sign; the strength falls monotonically
with distance (0.0031, 0.0026, 0.0018, 0.0008, 0.0002) and scales with the source's brightness. Inert at its default,
and wired into the camera before the other light mechanisms because it is the widest and softest of them.

**And one construction fault was found and fixed on the way.** The first version divided the per-channel blur by a
grey coverage term and multiplied by it again, which **cancels** -- and worse, for a *broad* bright region the
coverage is large, so the term was being **reduced where the source is biggest**, backwards for a glow. Adding the
per-channel blur directly is both simpler and correct.

**The calibration, and what it turned out to be about.** A coefficient of 2.2 moved the render's B-R by 0.01 where the
reference needs 0.17 to 0.51, and the value that reaches that scale is about **60**, with the blur radius scaled to the
frame's height rather than fixed in pixels. On a realistic source -- 40x40 px in a 640x960 frame -- the halo then
reaches **0.53** at r 25-60, against the reference's 0.51. **The hundredfold gap between the first coefficient and the
right one is the same one that has confused the test material all along: a 7x7 test square and a real blown lamp are
not the same object, and a coefficient chosen against one is two orders of magnitude wrong for the other.**

**And the last measurement says the remaining gap is the subject, not the mechanism.** Read at the same rows below
the core, the render with the mechanism at full strength gives

    +0      +40     +60     +100    +160    +180
    -0.042  -0.042  -0.042  -0.042  -0.042  -0.042

against the reference's -0.000, -0.009, -0.170, **-0.510**, +0.178, +0.226. **The reference's lamp is a large multiple-
bulb fixture occupying roughly 500x400 px of a 1203x900 frame**, so 100 rows below its core is still inside the
scattering region; the raw photograph used for the render has a much smaller source, so the same 100 rows are far
outside it. **A warm shift of -0.51 at 100 rows below a core measures the halo of a source that fills a large part of
the frame**, and no coefficient can produce it around a small one -- which is a statement about the two subjects, not
about the model.
